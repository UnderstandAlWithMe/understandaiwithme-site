A profitable business can still run out of cash. Customers pay late, a big supplier bill lands in the same week as payroll, and suddenly the account is empty even though the year looks fine on paper. A 13-week cash flow forecast is the simplest way to see those weeks coming.

Thirteen weeks is roughly one quarter. It is short enough to be fairly accurate and long enough to give you time to act.

*We are not financial advisers. This guide is about organizing your own numbers. For decisions about borrowing or funding, speak to your accountant or a qualified adviser.*

## What the forecast looks like

It is one spreadsheet with weeks across the top and money down the side:

- **Opening balance:** the cash in the bank at the start of the week.
- **Money in:** customer payments you expect, by week.
- **Money out:** wages, rent, suppliers, loan repayments, tax, subscriptions.
- **Closing balance:** opening balance plus money in, minus money out. This becomes next week's opening balance.

The line that matters is the closing balance. Any week where it drops below the cushion you are comfortable with is a week to act on now.

## Step-by-step: build your first forecast

### 1. Start with today's bank balance

Use the real balance in your business account today, not what your accounting software says you are owed.

### 2. List money coming in, by the week you will actually receive it

Go through your unpaid invoices and put each one in the week you realistically expect payment, not the due date. If a customer usually pays two weeks late, forecast it two weeks late. Add regular income you are confident about, such as retainers or subscriptions. Leave out sales you hope for but have not won.

### 3. List money going out

Work through your last two or three months of bank statements and pull out every regular payment:

- Wages and contractor payments
- Rent and utilities
- Supplier bills
- Loan or finance repayments
- Software subscriptions
- Tax payments (check the dates with your accountant or your tax authority)

Put each one in the week it leaves your account.

### 4. Calculate the closing balance for each week

The formula is simple: opening + money in - money out = closing. Each closing balance carries to the next week.

### 5. Mark the danger weeks

Highlight any week where the closing balance falls below your minimum cushion. Pick a cushion that would cover at least one payroll or one month of fixed costs, whatever feels safe for your business.

## Let AI do the spreadsheet work

You do not need to be good with formulas. ChatGPT and Claude can both build the sheet for you.

### Prompt to build the template

*"Build a 13-week cash flow forecast spreadsheet for a small business. Weeks as columns starting on Monday [date]. Rows: opening balance, money in (customer payments, other income), money out (wages, rent, suppliers, loan repayments, tax, subscriptions, other), total in, total out, closing balance. Closing balance should carry to the next week's opening balance. Highlight any closing balance below [amount] in red. Give me a downloadable Excel file."*

### Prompt to fill it from your data

Export your unpaid invoices and recent bank transactions (remove account numbers first), upload them and ask:

*"Using these files, list the regular payments going out and when they usually happen. Then place each unpaid invoice in the forecast, assuming each customer pays as late as they did on average in the past. Show me your assumptions in a separate list so I can check them."*

Always read the assumptions list. AI can misread a one-off payment as a regular one, or miss an annual bill.

## Keep it updated weekly

A forecast is only useful if it is current. Once a week, on the same day:

1. Replace the forecast for last week with what actually happened.
2. Update the opening balance to today's real bank balance.
3. Move any late customer payments to a later week.
4. Add a new week at the end so you always see 13 weeks ahead.

This takes around 15 minutes once the sheet exists.

## What to do when you see a danger week

Fix the timing first:

- Send invoices the day the work is done.
- Set up automatic payment reminders.
- Ask regular customers for deposits or staged payments on bigger jobs.
- Talk to suppliers early about payment dates.

If you still need funding, compare the total cost of each option, not just the monthly repayment, and get advice before you sign.

## The short version

- A 13-week forecast shows the weeks when cash will run short.
- Put money in and out in the week it actually moves, not when it is due.
- Let ChatGPT or Claude build the sheet, but always check its assumptions.
- Update it every week in about 15 minutes.
- Fix timing first, then look at funding. We are not financial advisers.
